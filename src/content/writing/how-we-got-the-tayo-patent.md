For years, I had to keep quiet about one of the most formative projects I worked on while the patent process was underway.

Our **Assistive Physical Therapy Powered Table**—developed under the TAYÔ Project at DLSU EDA-IBEHT in collaboration with DLSMHSI—has officially been granted a Philippine Invention Patent (**1/2023/050418 B1**, filed 3 August 2023 and granted 8 May 2026). I am grateful to be listed as one of its co-inventors.

Looking back across years of prototyping, troubleshooting, and iteration, two lessons stand out most clearly.

## 1. Innovation starts with knowing and empathizing with the target user

In rehabilitation robotics, getting a mechanism to move is the fun part—and also just the starting line. Our table was built to support a patient's trunk, upper legs, and lower legs while transitioning across supine, sitting, lift, and standing positions.

Moving from early concepts to later versions taught me quickly that you cannot design in a bubble. Sitting down with patients and physical therapists to understand their daily workflow, how patient transfers really happen, and what makes a session safe and comfortable is what turns a basic engineering mechanism into a novel, patentable solution.

## 2. Collaboration and communication across institutions unlock real breakthroughs

This project put engineers, clinicians, academic researchers, IP specialists, and industry partners in the same room. Each group speaks a completely different language and worries about different risks.

Learning to communicate clearly and listen across those worlds surfaced insights that no single team could have reached alone. That shared problem-solving—sustained over years of prototyping, safety testing, and iteration—is how we arrived at an invention worth protecting.

## A milestone, not the end of the story

A patent is an important R&D milestone, and it also reveals how much more it takes to bridge university research into real-world adoption. Seeing that gap firsthand is a big part of why I pursued project management, product thinking, and now doctoral research in fundamental rehabilitation sciences.
