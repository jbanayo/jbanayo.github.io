One of the things I enjoyed most about working in medical robotics was discovering how many questions appear *after* a prototype starts working.

In R&D, getting a mechanism to move is a meaningful milestone. But turning that idea into something people could actually use asks much more of a team:

- Does it fit the therapist's actual workflow?
- Is it comfortable and reassuring for the person using it?
- What happens if a component fails?
- Can we manufacture and maintain it consistently?
- If software handles patient information, how do we protect that data and make it useful where it needs to go?
- What evidence, approvals, and institutional paths are needed before wider use?

Working on TAYÔ taught me to see those questions as part of the product itself rather than afterthoughts. Medical, engineering, software, legal, IP, and business perspectives each revealed constraints and possibilities that were easy to miss from a single discipline.

It also changed how I manage projects. I learned to ask which risks need attention first, which decisions depend on evidence we do not yet have, and which improvements can wait. Prioritization was never purely technical—it meant communicating clearly with clinicians, engineers, researchers, and stakeholders who reasonably saw urgency differently.

That is the part of building assistive technology I find most exciting: making thoughtful tradeoffs, learning across disciplines, and steadily moving promising research closer to something that genuinely helps people in real life.
